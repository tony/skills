# Concept sets

What counts as a concept set, how to classify and record one, and how a
catalog is compared against the sets a study finds.

## What counts

A concept set is a grouping an identifiable source presents as coherent, with
named members and an explainable relation among them: Maslow's five needs,
Erikson's eight stages, Kelly's eleven corollaries. "Concept set" is this
skill's descriptive label, not a standard term.

The standard umbrella is **concept system**, which ISO 1087:2019 defines as a
"set of concepts structured in one or more related domains according to the
concept relations among its concepts"
([NIST CSRC glossary](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/concept_system)).
Knowledge organization uses "knowledge organization system (KOS), concept
system, or concept scheme" as one generic term
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_organization_system)), and
the broad sense of KOS reaches "conceptual systems, theories, disciplines,
cultures" (Mazzocchi, [IEKO "Knowledge organization system"](https://www.isko.org/cyclo/kos),
§3). A theory's named sets are concept systems in that broad sense.

Record a set only when a source presents it as a set: a named grouping, at
least two named members, and a relation the source states or a reader can
explain from it. Leave out lists with no attributable origin, narrative
theories that never enumerate their parts, glossaries and other alphabetical
term lists, and continuous models with no named discrete regimes.

A grouping assembled by the person compiling a catalog, rather than by any
source, is an editorial grouping. It can be a useful entry; record it with
`provenance: editorial` so it is never mistaken for an author's system.

## Framework, set, member

A framework holds sets, a set holds members, and a member can head a further
set: Dialectical Behavior Therapy > skills modules > Mindfulness > "what"
skills > Observe. Each set is its own row, linked to the set above it by
`parent`. A search that stops at the framework misses every set below it.

SKOS gives the same distinction for the representation you build. It defines
"meaningful groupings or 'collections' of concepts", a `skos:OrderedCollection`
for a collection whose order matters, and treats a collection's name as a node
label, which "does not represent a label for a concept in its own right"
([W3C SKOS Primer](https://www.w3.org/TR/2009/NOTE-skos-primer-20090818/),
§4.1). Keep the set, its name, and the concepts it groups apart.

## Kinds

The kind says how the members relate. Pick the one the source supports; when
the source mixes two, pick the dominant one and say so in `open`.

- `typology`: alternative kinds of one thing (identity statuses, cognitive
  distortions). When the source states the kinds are mutually exclusive and
  cover the whole, the set is a value partition: subclasses "mutually
  exclusive" that "completely cover the parent class"
  ([W3C, Representing Specified Values in OWL](https://www.w3.org/TR/2005/NOTE-swbp-specified-values-20050517/)).
  Classification arranges non-overlapping classes while categorization
  groups loosely with fuzzy boundaries (Wikipedia, above); record overlap the
  source admits in `open` instead of claiming exclusivity.
- `stage`: an ordered developmental sequence (psychosocial stages).
- `process`: ordered procedural steps (the ABCDE model). Nilsen separates
  process models, which "recognize a temporal sequence", from determinant
  frameworks, which do not
  ([Implementation Science 10:53, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4406164/));
  the same test separates `process` from `componential`.
- `level`: ordered by rank, priority, or depth (hierarchy of needs).
- `componential`: unordered parts of one whole (basic psychological needs).
- `skills`: capacities or practices grouped for learning (DBT modules).
- `cross-classification`: members generated by crossing two or more axes, a
  2x2 or larger; the row names its axis sets in `axes`, each its own row.
- `axiom`: propositions, postulates, or corollaries. Each member carries its
  `substance`, the proposition itself, not only its short label.

## Order

`order` records why the members come in the sequence they do:
`developmental` (growth over a life or a group's history), `procedural` (steps
someone carries out), `rank` (priority or depth), or `presentation` (the order
the source lists them in, with no claim behind it). Keep members in the
source's order and wording whatever the value.

## Where to look

Sweep by school and period before sweeping by phrase. Hjørland notes of
behaviorism, cognitivism, psychoanalysis, and activity theory that "each of
these theories implies its own subject matter for psychology and hence its own
conceptual system and classification" ([IEKO "Classification of psychology"](https://www.isko.org/cyclo/psychology.htm),
§4). Any field with rival schools works the same way, so list the schools
first and search each.

Search with phrasing that surfaces every kind, never one word for all of them:
`"stages of"`, `"phases of"`, `"types of"`, `"levels of"`, `"model of"`,
`"the <N> <nouns> of"` with plausible numbers and member nouns, `typology`,
`taxonomy`, `classification`, `principles of`, `corollaries`. The word
"taxonomy" alone drops stage and process models, and Gilchrist found it "used
with at least five diverse (but overlapping) meanings" (cited in Mazzocchi,
§4.2.2).

For a knowledge organization topic itself, the ISKO proceedings series
[Advances in Knowledge Organization](https://portal.isko.org/Advances-in-Knowledge-Organization)
is a primary literature source; volume 12 is *Paradigms and conceptual systems
in knowledge organization* (2010).

## sets.jsonl

One JSON object per line, one line per set. Every field is required; use an
empty string, an empty list, or `null` where the field says so.

- `schema`: `1`.
- `id`: a lowercase hyphenated slug, unique in the study.
- `name`: the source's own name for the set. A name you supply goes in
  `open` as "descriptive title supplied by the study".
- `aliases`: other names in use, including author-name forms
  ("Maslow's hierarchy").
- `parent`: the id of the set this one sits under, or `""`.
- `variant_of`: the id of the earlier version this one revises, or `""`. A
  revision, a competing count, or an adaptation is its own row.
- `framework`: the theory, school, or body of work the set belongs to.
- `author`, `year`: the originating author or body, and the year of the
  version this row records. When no source you read gives that year, write
  `unknown` and name the unread source in `open`; never a retrieval or
  access year.
- `purpose`: what the set lets someone distinguish, describe, explain, or
  predict, in one sentence.
- `member_noun`: the source's noun for its members (stages, statuses, needs,
  modes, corollaries).
- `stated_count`: the count the source states, or `null` when it states none.
- `members`: objects in the source's order, each with `label` (the source's
  wording), `gloss` (a short explanation, `""` when not yet written), and,
  for `axiom` sets, `substance`. Optional `aliases` holds other labels for
  the member.
- `kind`, `order`: from the closed sets above.
- `axes`: for `cross-classification` only, the ids of the axis sets.
- `standing`: `canonical`, `contested`, `superseded`, or `popularized` (in
  wide use beyond the source, often reshaped).
- `provenance`: `original` (the author's own formulation), `later-synthesis`
  (a later author assembled it from the original), or `editorial` (no source
  presents it as a set).
- `cites`: objects with `source` (a `source` value from `sources.jsonl`),
  `locator`, `quote`, `role` (`primary` for the originating text, `secondary`
  for a textbook, review, or encyclopedia), and `url` when the source has
  one. Locators follow `citation.md`; a book page needs the edition recorded
  as the source's `ref`.
- `open`: what is unresolved, one string each: members not recovered, a
  primary source not reached, overlap the source admits, a disputed date.

When `stated_count` exceeds the members recovered, `open` must say so. When
there are more members than the stated count, the larger enumeration is a
different version and gets its own row. When no cite is `primary`, `open`
names the primary source that was not reached.

## catalog.jsonl

The reader's existing catalog, one entry per line, in the catalog's own
wording. Write it from the catalog file or pasted list without correcting it;
the diff exists to find what it gets wrong.

- `entry`: the entry's text.
- `members`: the members the catalog shows for it, as strings, or `[]` for a
  single concept.
- `stated_count`: a count the entry claims ("Ekman's list of fifteen"), or
  `null`.
- `source`: the source the catalog gives for the grouping, or `""`.
- `matches`: the ids of the sets this entry records. Leave the field out to
  match by name and alias; write it, even as `[]`, when the match needs
  judgment. Match only when the entry and the set name the same grouping from
  the same source; a shared word is not a match.

## What the diff reports

`concept_sets.py diff` sorts what the catalog has not codified into six
categories:

- `uncodified`: sourced sets (provenance not `editorial`) that no entry
  records.
- `loose_members`: single-concept entries that are members of an uncodified
  set: the catalog holds the parts but not the system.
- `partial`: entries whose stated count exceeds the members they show, that
  lack members their matched set names (`missing`), or that show members the
  set does not name (`extra`, often a member borrowed from another version).
- `unsourced_groupings`: entries that group two or more members with no source,
  where the only matching sets are editorial or none match.
- `merged_versions`: entries matched to two versions of one set, linked by
  `variant_of`.
- `unknown_matches`: entries naming a set id the study lacks; fix the
  catalog row before reading the rest.

## Terms to avoid

These labels circulate in chat-assistant answers about this task. None is
supported in the knowledge organization literature in the sense offered, so
use the sourced terms above instead.

- "Domain micro-KOS", "compendium of canonical frameworks", "enumerated
  conceptual model", and "named enumerated framework": no knowledge
  organization source uses them.
- "Theories, models and frameworks" (TMFs): Nilsen's taxonomy of five
  categories for implementation science, not a cross-disciplinary label for
  enumerated sets.
- "Enumerative classification": in knowledge organization, a scheme that lists
  its classes in advance, as the Library of Congress Classification is
  "essentially enumerative" (Mazzocchi, §4.1.3), not a closed list of members.
